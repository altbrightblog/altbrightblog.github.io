Les Visiteurs du Soir

I watched it recently.

The casting in this film is truly impeccable. Dominique, Gilles, and the Devil—each of them looks unmistakably like a demon rather than an ordinary human being.

First of all, Dominique and Gilles both have an overall appearance that feels highly androgynous, almost hermaphroditic and mirror-like, whether in the actors’ own presence or in the way the characters are conceived. Yet this does not remain at the literal level of sexual ambiguity: both their narrative functions and their visual qualities are portrayed as extending far beyond ordinary male and female gender.

Dominique’s face, presence, gaze, and manner of speaking—for example, at the very beginning—all make it clear that she could never be the demon who would betray the Devil. Her entire being resembles a devil: an incarnation of seduction and destruction, as well as a highly competent operative. She knows exactly what she is doing, understands how to exploit men’s weaknesses and jealousy, watches how others are controlled by desire, and never grows soft-hearted when the situation begins to collapse.

Gilles, however, is different. Although his presence is strange, exotic, and plainly not of this world, he lacks Dominique’s predatory quality. To me, he seems quieter, more melancholy, and faintly detached, like a ghost. He actually appears delicate, almost like someone who wandered into the Devil’s camp by mistake. (The fact that he helps that man at the beginning, while Dominique reacts with contempt, already foreshadows that he will genuinely fall in love with the target of his mission.) Alain Cuny’s beauty is precisely that of someone who does not belong to this world—extremely cold and distant—which is why the character of Gilles works so well. He begins as someone sent by the Devil, gradually reveals human emotions, and ultimately betrays his entire allegiance for love.

This is why one can tell from the characters alone that the film is a highly fantastical fairy tale: the casting is so good. Although the two of them initially carry out the same mission in mirrored ways, symbolically they are complete opposites. Gilles also never seems to enjoy manipulation and observation the way Dominique does; otherwise, his later love for Anne would feel extremely abrupt. At first, the two characters are intensely symmetrical—Gilles is even colder than I had imagined—but emotion later distinguishes them and sends their stories in different directions.

Because the film was made in 1942, when France was still under German occupation, I have seen people suggest that it contains a certain political allegory. I do not agree. To me, it is simply a fairy tale—a love story.

The Devil is not evil in the sense of being indiscriminately wicked, cruel, or monstrous. On the contrary, I find him rather elegant and articulate. He also possesses the flustered, humiliated anger of someone whose beloved does not love him back and whose subordinate has disobeyed his orders.

All of this is simply far too much like a fairy tale for me to connect it with reality.

Dominique is a calm and professional operative, whereas the Devil is a jealous figure of authority. In the end, he turns Gilles and Anne into stone statues, yet I almost feel that he grants them fulfilment, because in this form they become eternal. It is not as though everything ceases to exist once human life ends. That may be the conventional understanding, but I believe love can transcend life. Besides, even after he transforms them into stone, their hearts continue to beat. Ultimately, the Devil’s motivation is merely that he wants everyone to obey him. He cannot accept anything beyond his control. He only wants to intensify the latent conflict between two possessive older men until they destroy one another; he feels a certain managerial fury because his own garçon refuses to follow orders; and when Anne rejects him, his narcissism is wounded, provoking humiliation and rage. The central issue is not, “I cannot possess this woman,” but rather, “I can manipulate the great weaknesses of human nature—jealousy and desire—so why can I not command someone to truly love me?” Everything about this is profoundly fairy-tale-like. His evil does not consist of simply taking pleasure in invasion and slaughter. That is why I do not find him particularly wicked.

My favourite image in the film is the fountain. The first time I saw it, I immediately decided that it was important and would undoubtedly return at the end. In fairy tales and Gothic stories, significant objects are almost never mere decoration; they are emotional mechanisms. (The music box in Dracula (2025), for example.) Their first appearance establishes meaning, while their second fulfils it. The moment I saw the fountain, I knew it had to be connected to their love. It is so romantic.

The disadvantages, however, are equally obvious: the narrative pace is far too slow. The film is very old, so I understand that its pacing may have been heavily influenced by the theatrical conventions popular at the time. Yet viewed today, it still feels excessively drawn out. (At times I even wondered whether it was in slow motion...)

In truth, controlling the rhythm of a film is often extremely difficult. Take Call Me by Your Name, for example: the film spends enough time developing the ambiguity and attraction between the two men, yet endlessly postpones any actual progression in their relationship. This is precisely why I do not particularly like film as a medium. I have encountered very few films whose narrative pacing genuinely satisfies me. So far, only The Shawshank Redemption and Léon have done so.

Then I read the screenplay, and I felt that this story was genuinely made to be written as a short story... If it were a work of fiction, the pacing would be perfect.

The truly powerful part of a work is the moment when its conflicts erupt.

I find the pacing of many films extremely sluggish because the first half is tasked with establishing the world and the relationships, while the second half suddenly accelerates in order to accommodate both the conflict and the limited running time. Perhaps some screenwriters become too absorbed while writing and begin treating the screenplay like a novel, losing sight of structure. The problem is that audiences usually understand the intended relationships and world very quickly, because they are highly attentive at the beginning of a film. Conversely, when the opening itself drags, I lose interest no matter how good the later sections become.

This seems to be a very typical three-act screenwriting structure:

The first act is often excessively slow because the screenwriter cannot bear to remove world-building or character exposition. In this film, for example, an enormous amount of the opening is spent showing how enclosed, dreamlike, and magnificent the castle is... Yet none of this introduces character conflict or truly advances the plot.

In the second act, the conflicts gradually emerge and the story begins to become interesting, but most works fail to judge when the second act should properly begin.

The third act must then conclude everything within a very limited amount of time, so the pacing suddenly becomes as fast as a roller coaster, as though someone were chasing the story from behind.

I believe a work can contain profound and extensive groundwork. That is, of course, a good thing—certainly better than throwing lines of dialogue at the audience to explain what kind of person someone is and what has happened to them. Otherwise, why make a film at all? The crucial point, however, is to transform that groundwork into pressure that continually escalates the story, with each beat locking into the next. Even in a slow-paced work where almost nothing visibly advances, one should still be able to feel that something is steadily approaching.

This film, however, gives me the impression that the first act keeps circling in place, while the second act abruptly begins rushing to catch up.

After watching this film, I became even more determined to become a novelist first: to write the stories I want to tell in concrete form, and only then adapt them into screenplays.

The narrative structures of many great films remain stable not merely because “the screenplay is well written,” but because a novelistic completeness exists behind them first. The Shawshank Redemption, Forrest Gump—although it is merely an ordinary American Dream story—and The Godfather are examples.

A novel naturally resolves several questions:

Why do the characters change? Why do they form relationships? What effect does the world have upon them? Which details concern the characters, and which concern fate?

Take Forrest Gump as an example. Although the content of the story is extraordinarily bizarre, it does not progress through a conventional three-act structure. Instead, one character travels through an era while fragments of a life continually accumulate around him. If a screenwriter attempted to create this kind of story directly for film, it could easily become scattered because the centre of gravity would be difficult to identify. A novel can more easily sustain this wandering, dreamlike, memory-driven structure, after which a screenwriter can select, compress, and reorganise the material. This does not mean, of course, that films adapted from novels are necessarily good. Screenwriting is a process of selecting images and scenes, and I think it places even greater demands on the writer’s skill.

My response after reading this film’s screenplay was that it is merely a short story formatted as a screenplay; it never truly transforms its beautiful narrative into cinematic language. The greatest difference between fiction and screenwriting is that the novel determines the soul, while the screenplay determines the body and skeleton. Because film is constrained by running time, every shot must be calculated with precision.

Then there are the typography, transitions, and visual effects to complain about. The film opens by turning the pages of a storybook, which actually creates a rather convincing fairy-tale atmosphere. But all the lettering is in Gothic type. Although this is a Gothic story, I still find Gothic lettering excessively ornate—Le Monde’s current logo, for example. I prefer typography that is clean and highly legible. Of course, this was partly limited by the technology of the period. The effects and transitions are also extremely clumsy for technical reasons, rather like PowerPoint, haha. When technology is severely limited, the story, atmosphere, and casting become even more important. Consider many present-day Netflix series: the costumes and sets may be magnificent, and one can certainly say that the production design is beautiful, but there is nothing else of value.

Ah, what a beautiful Gothic love story. It is unbearably romantic... It is also a beautiful fairy tale for adults. The words the two lovers use when confessing their feelings are so awkward, innocent, and stubborn, as though even the destruction of the world could not separate them. They possess a courage and certainty unavailable to ordinary worldly people. To me, this is the essence of an adult fairy tale, because very few people retain such innocent stubbornness and courage. Most are merely worldly and calculating. The essence of both courage and fairy tales is that a person understands the cruelty of the world and still refuses to alter their love.

Why are Gothic stories so compelling? Because they always place love within extreme circumstances, making everything feel extraordinarily weighty rather than frivolous.

Ordinary romance films merely repeat, again and again, the dull and foolish things that the dull majority perform in everyday life and nevertheless insist on calling love.
