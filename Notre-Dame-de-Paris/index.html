Notre-Dame de Paris

2026-07-12 13 min read # Literature # Comment

Yesterday, I reread Notre-Dame de Paris, one of my favorite literary works. I first read it when I was ten. Yesterday, I wrote down some thoughts on X.

La première fois que j’ai lu Notre-Dame de Paris, je me suis dit : mais cette femme est vraiment stupide, qu’est-ce qui ne va pas chez elle ? Phœbus est clairement un playboy, non ? Alors qu’à côté, il y a ce prêtre, cet homme plus âgé, tellement beau, rempli de savoir,…

— Alt Bright (@altArtist7) July 11, 2026

The common reading is usually this: Esmeralda is the most innocent; she is too naive about love. Quasimodo’s love is the purest. Frollo is the most evil and disgusting villain. Phoebus is the most frivolous. So this is Esmeralda’s tragedy.

But I don’t think so. Although I admit that I have an extremely strong bias toward older men, and this book was also my initiation into loving older men, hahahahahahaha!!! I still think the tragedy of this book should be Frollo’s.

From beginning to end, I absolutely do not think the protagonist of this book is Esmeralda, because it is very clear that the author shaped her as a woman-as-object, and as a hollow functional character, because the text basically does not spend much space describing her in depth at all (compared with Frollo). Of course, the same is true of Quasimodo, which I will mention later. To a great extent, she is written as an object “to be looked at, desired, fought over, and executed.” The difference between an object and a subject lies in the thinness or thickness of subjectivity. Clearly, her subjectivity is very thin. What she carries is mostly function, for example: making Phoebus frivolous, making Quasimodo produce “pure love,” making Frollo go mad, and making the whole of society reveal its true face as a tragic machine that crushes people.

In other words, this character is of course not a complete person, but a device that makes the men and the social machine become visible.

So I have always felt that she is not the protagonist at all. The real protagonist is in the hidden line. I have always felt that she is extremely hollow. Why do people think she is the protagonist? Because she is the center of the plot. So the structure of this book is one that is extremely easy for some contemporary feminists to attack: woman as object, man as subject. The woman is the center of all desire, but the men have most of the complete narrative.

But I am also extremely opposed to that kind of extreme view. Because in this book, the only person who truly has subjectivity is Frollo. Everyone else is an object. So you can’t really drag feminism into this. (Of course, in some so-called strong-female-lead works, men also exist as objects, and I could also say that this is unfair to men. So I don’t want to discuss gender issues.)

There is another point I find extremely hard to understand. Based on life experience, Esmeralda should not really be this kind of pure little white flower who is so innocent that she severely lacks the ability to judge people… because she has been performing on the streets of Paris for a long time, and the people she comes into contact with are all kinds of marginal figures. Someone who makes a living through her own body and the gaze of the audience — shouldn’t she know very clearly who is merely lustful, who just wants to take advantage of her, who is sincere, and whose words are complete bullshit?

And then Phoebus appears. He has a bit of an officer’s aura, and somehow she falls in love with him…

That’s why I wrote that post on X, hahaha.

What I had originally expected was another version: she understands men very well, and only wants to have some fun with Phoebus, but would never hand herself over. In the end, she realizes that although that dangerous older priest is dangerous and possessive, he possesses real knowledge, the weight of love, and sustained attention. If this story had a female lead with a brain, it would immediately be upgraded from a tragedy into a romantic Gothic love story.

Then I did some searching, and it seems that in nineteenth-century narrative, this kind of typical Romantic arrangement is very visible. It is still what I said earlier: she seems to have to be shaped into a very divided object in order to appear innocent enough, and in order to make the whole of society appear extremely unjust. But the problem is, when the symbolism is too heavy, the character becomes distorted. And I don’t find it that romantic either.

As for Quasimodo, the common reading is that his love is extremely pure, and the ending of the story is usually interpreted as “the purest person finally reunites with the person he loves in death,” but I don’t think that at all.

Because from the perspective of personal structure, he has the capacity to love, but he has not undergone enough socialization to become a complete person. If the person has not even grown into a complete person, then where does this thing called “love” even come from?

If you don’t understand, I’ll give the most ordinary example: a child who is close to adulthood but not yet truly eighteen, still studying in school, falls in love with a man more than twenty years older who has already been working for a long time. Of course a child has the capacity to love someone. (I can put it this way: everyone has the capacity to love someone. The only question is whether he is willing to pour that love into you, or whether he loves himself more.) But I do not think that, at this stage, his love is something that can be accepted by a fully socialized person. The reason is that although he has experienced the small society of school, he has not experienced the larger real society. So of course he is still incomplete as a person. And if he is incomplete, where does love come from? Socialization does not count simply because there are people around you.

The same is true of Quasimodo. He has the capacity to love, but he has never received sufficient socialization. His world consists almost entirely of Notre-Dame, the bells, and Frollo. He has long been humiliated and rejected, so he severely lacks equal human relationships and is also excluded from the entire world. His physical deformity further isolates him from all this (and also deepens his inferiority complex), so he has almost no chance to be fully socialized, nor does he know how to love another person as an independent subject.

Whether you love this person, or love their function, or allow them to satisfy your projection — these are very different things.

A person can develop extremely intense feelings for someone who sees them and helps them while living in severe isolation, but that does not mean it is mature love. What love requires is a complete subject.

So it is very clear that Quasimodo’s feelings for Esmeralda do contain protection and sacrifice, of course, but a large part of them also very obviously consists of “the shock of being treated kindly for the first time, idealization, attachment to the helper, and projection onto the object of salvation.” That has very little to do with love.

So this is how I read the ending: it is not the completion of mutual love between the two of them, but the permanent freezing of his one-sided attachment through death. To put it harshly, he voluntarily dies beside Esmeralda’s corpse, while she never chose him. Two skeletons embracing each other does not mean love has been established. It only means that, in the end, he attached himself to that symbol. I find it extremely repulsive to package emotional attachment caused by extreme deprivation as the purest form of love.

Now, as for Phoebus. He is also an object, but unlike Quasimodo.

In my eyes, he is a cog in the huge machine of society, one that has already been programmed to rotate a certain number of times. He looks very socialized, because he knows how to flirt, how to use his identity as an officer, how to switch between an engagement and a romantic adventure, and how to play with other people.

But that does not mean he has a strong sense of subjectivity. Quite the opposite: he is merely executing the role society has handed to him extremely well. Because he has never really decided anything. He simply moves along the track. He has almost no self-reflection, no value conflict, and is not changed by relationships. So he is a cog that drives the plot inside the huge machine of society, a machine capable of crushing people. Whatever program society writes for him, he follows that program. The most ironic thing is that people like this seem to live quite well in reality.

This story is actually the story of an older man, the tragedy of an older man. The protagonist of this story should be Frollo.

First of all, I do not think he is the villain. The public usually thinks this because, on the level of action, he does indeed commit the clearest acts of harm. We are not discussing right or wrong here. I think there are no heroes or villains in this story; everyone has their own position, so no one should be written merely as a villain. If one must say who the villain is, I think it is Phoebus, because Frollo consciously loses control, whereas Phoebus does not. One looks like he is going mad; the other looks like a machine. Why can a society crush people? Because the numb cogs inside the social machine never stop turning, while the mad people merely accelerate the process of crushing someone.

This book gives most of its spiritual resources to Frollo: for example, it describes his psychology and his history in great detail. Only because of this do we know that he is also the only complex subject in the book who possesses knowledge, authority, history, self-awareness, desire, conflict, and collapse. So this story is actually his subjective tragedy. The other objects mostly perform functions within the mechanism of tragedy.

So one could say that the others make the story happen, while Frollo makes the story become literature. And on top of everything I said on X, he is simply too handsome!!! Mon Dieu!!!!!!

But why does his tragedy happen? This is also what I often say: literary works usually do not allow older men who truly know how to love to receive someone worthy of that kind of mad, burning love. Instead, they always write older men into extremely miserable endings. Frollo is clearly the person in the entire book who understands most deeply what love can change, but he loved the wrong person!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!! Just like the Phantom in The Phantom of the Opera, he loved a young person who was both foolish and completely unworthy of him. That is the real tragedy.

Of course, in this book, the older man is not tragic only because he cannot obtain the person he loves. Rather, his tragedy lies in the fact that, for the first time, he discovers that after falling in love with someone, he cannot control himself — yet he has also never learned how to control himself. In the end, he drags everyone into the abyss.

That is what a tragedy really is.
