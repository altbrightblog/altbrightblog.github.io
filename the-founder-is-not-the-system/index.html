The Founder Is Not the System

2026-09-18 7 min read # Business Strategy # Corporate Governance # Apple # Tesla # Meta # Silicon Valley # Tech # Elon Musk # Thought

Last night, I was talking with ChatGPT about a question: what happens when a publicly listed company becomes heavily dependent on a single person?

I think about this quite often. I find founder-led companies deeply compelling, and I genuinely like this kind of company. But the structural problems they can create are exactly what I cannot help worrying about.

Then ChatGPT, somewhat inexplicably, cited Tesla's Elon Musk and Meta's Mark Zuckerberg as examples to suggest that I was worrying too much. To me, that is precisely where ChatGPT's reasoning felt old-fashioned. These two companies are not counterexamples; they are among the clearest examples of the very risks that need to be avoided.

Above is Tesla's 2025 annual report. The section I highlighted is Tesla itself explicitly acknowledging the company's dependence on Elon's services, along with related concerns about employees and talent.

The relationship between Elon and Tesla has long gone beyond that of a CEO and a company. It has taken on an almost quasi-religious character: the personal cult around Elon, the futurist narrative, and the way so much attention to Tesla is inseparable from him. Without Elon, the number of people who remain interested in the company would almost certainly be much smaller. The same applies to Tesla's share price: when Elon says something erratic on social media, the market can visibly react. The Tesla-Elon relationship is an extreme case. It is not a model that cultural companies should imitate, and even technology companies should be wary of copying it, because it represents an obvious key-person risk.

Meta presents a different problem. Its underlying model is essentially: accumulate a huge base of long-time users -> sell advertising.

Zuckerberg is, of course, far more low-key than Elon. Meta's current position reminds me of a Chinese saying: 'A starving camel is still bigger than a horse.' Facebook gave the company an enormous early user base and, with it, very strong cash flow. So even if Meta struggles to create genuinely new things, it is not going to disappear. But how many people actively use Facebook now? Meta increasingly relies on Instagram and WhatsApp. It also launched Threads, an X-like product, but its adoption is nowhere near dominant, and the fact that registration is tied to Instagram arguably makes the ecosystem even more closed.

Meta's software also frustrates me. The company clearly prioritizes mobile and seems to have largely neglected the web. The interfaces feel old and barely refreshed, especially Instagram on the web. What bothers me most is how unfriendly Meta can feel to new users. I have had at least five Instagram accounts suspended. Even if the goal is to prevent spam or advertising abuse, users should not be subjected to such a long, invisible and opaque probation period. People cannot be expected to use a product with the anxiety of sitting an exam. That is genuinely how Meta products - especially Instagram and Facebook - feel to me. Every day I feel as if I am walking a tightrope: cautiously following people I like, cautiously liking their posts, and then one day discovering that an account I really liked has suddenly been banned. Hahaha.

So Meta is like an enormous camel: the infrastructure, cash flow, users and advertising business are all still there, which makes it very hard to knock over. But it feels old, both in its growth narrative and in the character of its products. Instagram remains powerful, yet its center of gravity keeps drifting toward TikTok-style short video, namely Reels. Short-form video is obviously a major trend - YouTube pushes Shorts heavily as well - but were Instagram's real strengths not posts and Stories? Why stop continuously improving what made the product distinctive in the first place? WhatsApp also has major strategic value, but its monetization remains limited (I only recently realized it was owned by Meta, anyway). Messaging is not a Meta monopoly either: there is iMessage, and X Corp has launched X Chat. It is hard to know where that market is going, especially because Elon tends to pursue ideas impulsively, but the broader point remains: instant messaging is not a category Meta owns by default.

In other words, Meta's fundamental model is still the old one: advertising. It is profitable, certainly, but it also feels dated. It is like a game company that stops producing new games and instead remasters titles from more than twenty years ago every year. Meta now seems more focused on sustaining an old model than on defining, with the clarity, stability and continuity of Apple, what the next generation of its core product should be. That is why, even when the iPhone 15 Pro and 16 Pro are not dramatically different, I do not find Apple frustrating in the same way.

Apple has long been one of my favorite companies.

The most valuable lesson from Apple is not that 'a founder is forever irreplaceable.' It is whether a company can transform the founder's individual ability and charisma into institutions, product systems, durable operating capabilities and a deep talent pipeline. One of Tim Cook's greatest strengths is that he did not try to recreate Steve Jobs. Instead, he moved Apple from a founder-driven company toward a system-level company that had extracted and institutionalized the founder's spirit. The supply chain and operations became stronger; the product line continued to expand; and the brand, its remarkably consistent design standards, and the broader ecosystem did not disappear with the founder. That transition necessarily involved judgment, the refusal of some immediate temptations, and the willingness to let go of certain designs and ideas that had become outdated.

A truly healthy structure should work like this: the founder establishes the company's soul, history and DNA; the successor institutionalizes that soul. The founder's judgment, charisma, capabilities, project methods, aesthetic standards, brand credibility and symbolic weight are translated into systems the company can reproduce and sustain over time.

In other words, the founder's achievements and place in history can never be replaced by those who come after. But a founder can absolutely leave behind a structure that others are able to inherit.
